# DMARC & MTA-STS overview

Every monitored domain's **Overview** tab shows a health checklist: DMARC record, DMARC authorization record, TLS reporting record, MTA-STS, SPF, MX, DKIM, DNS provider. This page explains what each of these is actually protecting against and what needs to exist in DNS for it to go green - useful background before or alongside the hands-on setup in [Getting Started](./getting-started.mdx) and [MTA-STS](./mta-sts.mdx).

## SPF and DKIM

DMARC doesn't do anything on its own - it's a policy layer on top of two older mechanisms, and receivers use both to decide whether a message "authenticates" as being from your domain:

* **SPF** (RFC 7208) publishes which mail servers are allowed to send for your domain, as a `v=spf1 ...` TXT record at the domain apex. dotMARC's SPF check confirms exactly one such record exists and starts with the right prefix. It also counts the DNS lookups the record needs, following its includes, and flags a record that needs more than 10 (**Too many DNS lookups**) or includes a domain with no SPF record.
* **DKIM** (RFC 6376) signs outgoing mail with a private key, verified against a public key published at `<selector>._domainkey.<domain>`. Because selectors are provider-specific strings with no DNS-discoverable convention, dotMARC can't guess yours - the DKIM check only runs once you've told it which selector(s) to look for. If you've stored the record your mail platform gives you for a selector, the check also confirms DNS matches it.

A message can fail SPF or DKIM individually and still pass DMARC (it only needs one of the two to align with the visible From: domain) - which is exactly why DMARC aggregate reports are useful: they show you real-world pass/fail combinations you'd otherwise never see.

## The DMARC record

The record that turns monitoring on is a TXT record at `_dmarc.<domain>`:

```txt
v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.uk
```

* `p=` is the policy told to receivers for mail that fails SPF/DKIM alignment: `none` (monitor only), `quarantine` (send to spam), or `reject`. Most domains start at `none` and tighten once reports confirm legitimate senders all pass.
* `rua=` is where aggregate reports get sent. For dotMARC to see anything, this has to point at the mailbox address it polls (`Graph__MailboxAddress`).

dotMARC's **DMARC record** check confirms a record exists, starts with `v=DMARC1`, and that its `rua=` matches the configured mailbox - not just that a DMARC record exists at all.

### Record problems

dotMARC judges the record against [RFC 9989](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9989), the current DMARC standard. It replaced RFC 7489 in May 2026. The DMARC record check names each problem it finds, on the domain page under **Record problems**. There are two kinds.

**Breaking problems** are ones where receivers may ignore the record or read it as `p=none`. They set the check to Misconfigured, so the **DMARC record broken** alert covers them (see [DNS health alerts](./alerts.mdx#dns-health-alerts)).

| Problem | What receivers do |
| --- | --- |
| `v=DMARC1` missing, not first, or not exactly `DMARC1` | Ignore the whole record. |
| More than one DMARC record at `_dmarc.<domain>` | Discard all of them. A TXT value at that name is counted as a DMARC record when it starts with `v=DMARC1`. A value that only looks like one, because its `v` value starts with `DMARC` in any case (for example `v=dmarc1`) or it holds a `v=DMARC1` tag that is not first, is read as a DMARC record with a version problem and is not counted beside a proper record. Any other value, such as an SPF record or a verification token, is not DMARC and is ignored. |
| A tag that appears more than once | RFC 9989 does not say what to do with a repeated tag. dotMARC follows the DKIM tag-list rule that DMARC's syntax inherits (RFC 6376 section 3.2), under which the record is malformed, so receivers may ignore it. |
| No `p` tag, or a `p` that is not `none`, `quarantine` or `reject` | Treat the record as `p=none` when it has a valid `rua`, otherwise apply no DMARC processing (RFC 9989 section 4.10.1). |
| An invalid `sp` or `np` | The same: `p=none`, or no DMARC processing without a valid `rua`. |

**Informational problems** mean the record works but is out of date or not enforcing. They do not change the check status or raise an alert. They show on the domain page, and where they affect a [target](./baselines.mdx#meeting-a-target) they appear as the reason the domain is below it.

* `pct`, `rf` or `ri` present. RFC 9989 removed all three. Receivers that follow it ignore them, but older receivers still apply `pct`, so a `pct` below 100 counts against a target.
* `t=y`, testing mode. RFC 9989 added `t` (`y` or `n`, default `n`) to replace `pct=0`. With `t=y` the domain asks receivers not to apply the policy and to act one level lower, so `p=reject` is treated as `quarantine`. Turn it off once you are confident in the policy.

RFC 9989 also adds `np`, the policy for subdomains that do not exist. When a record has no `np`, those subdomains follow `sp`, and then `p`.

### Suggested record and repair

When a record has a problem, or sits below its target, the domain page shows a **Suggested record** with a Copy button and a line for each change. dotMARC's [DNS push](./dns-provider-push.mdx#repairing-a-dmarc-record) writes this record too, with dotMARC's report address added to `rua` if it is missing. The repair:

* puts `v=DMARC1` first and writes each tag once, keeping the other tags in their original order;
* keeps the strictest valid value of a repeated `p`, `sp` or `np`, so nothing a receiver might have applied is weakened, keeps `t=n` when `t` is repeated, joins repeated `rua` and `ruf` tags into one with each address once, and keeps the first value of any other repeated tag;
* writes a missing or invalid `p` as `p=none`, or as the strictest valid `p` found, and drops an invalid `sp` or `np` so it inherits;
* combines several DMARC records into one by the same rules, and leaves unrelated TXT values at the name alone;
* drops `rf` and `ri`, which have no effect on enforcement;
* keeps `pct` and `t` as they are when there is no target, because removing them would change enforcement without anyone asking. They stay flagged. With a target, `pct` and `t=y` are removed and `p`, `sp` and `np` are raised to it, never lowered.

### More than one `rua=` address

A record can list several report addresses, separated by commas. That is useful while you trial dotMARC beside another DMARC tool, so dotMARC reads the list rather than only the first address:

| What `rua=` lists | Status | Alerts? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Only dotMARC's mailbox | OK | No |
| dotMARC's mailbox and one other address | OK, other rua (information) | No |
| dotMARC's mailbox and two or more others | Too many rua addresses | Yes |
| Addresses that don't include dotMARC's mailbox | Misconfigured | Yes |
| Any address without the `mailto:` prefix | Misconfigured | Yes |

Two is the limit because receivers may cap how many report addresses they send to ([RFC 7489 §6.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7489#section-6.2)), and some only honour the first couple. With more than two, reports can stop reaching dotMARC for some senders without anything looking broken.

Every address needs its `mailto:` prefix, because `rua=` is a list of URIs. `rua=mailto:a@example.com,b@example.com` is wrong: receivers skip `b@example.com`, so that destination quietly receives nothing. dotMARC reports the record as Misconfigured and names the address.

**Push via your DNS provider** stays available for the information, too-many and misconfigured statuses. It shows you the change to confirm first, keeps every other tag, and writes `rua=` as dotMARC's mailbox first, then:

1. the **additional rua address**, if an admin has set one,
2. then any other addresses already in the record, in their original order, adding a missing `mailto:`,

up to the limit of two addresses in total. Two settings in the **DNS records** section of the DNS push settings page control this:

* **Keep other rua addresses when pushing** (on by default). Turn it off and a push writes dotMARC's mailbox, plus the additional address if there is one, and nothing else. Use that when you finish a trial and want the other address gone.
* **Additional rua address**. A second destination added to every DMARC record dotMARC pushes, for example to keep sending reports to another tool. If its domain differs from the domain being monitored, that domain must publish an authorization record (see below) or receivers won't send reports to it.

## The DMARC authorization record

This is the one that doesn't turn up much when you search for it, because it's not really called one thing: RFC 7489 §7.1 calls the mechanism **external destination verification**; other vendors call it a third-party authorization record. dotMARC's health checklist just calls it the "DMARC authorization record" since that's what it does - authorizes a report destination.

<details>
<summary>Why this record exists, and why it matters for dotMARC</summary>

**Why it exists:** anyone could put `rua=mailto:you@yourcompany.com` in *their* DMARC record, making every receiving mail server that enforces your domain's mail policy start emailing your mailbox aggregate reports about *their* domain. To stop that, the RFC requires the report *destination*'s domain to explicitly agree to receive reports for the *monitored* domain, whenever those two domains differ - by publishing its own TXT record:

```txt
<monitored-domain>._report._dmarc.<mailbox-domain> TXT "v=DMARC1"
```

**Why it matters for dotMARC specifically:** the normal shape here is one dotMARC deployment polling a single mailbox on the deployment's own domain (or an MSP's domain) while monitoring many customers' domains. That mailbox's domain will almost always differ from the domain being monitored - which means this record is required for essentially every domain you add, and without it, most receivers will simply decline to send that domain's reports to you at all, even though the domain's own `_dmarc` record looks perfectly correct.

</details>

If the mailbox's domain and the monitored domain are the same, dotMARC's check reports **Not applicable** and skips it entirely - the RFC doesn't require self-authorization. (dotMARC checks for an exact domain match rather than the RFC's broader "organizational domain" match; in practice this only matters if you're monitoring a subdomain of the mailbox's own domain, an unusual setup for this kind of deployment.)

When the check reports **Missing**, the domain's Overview tab shows a **Push authorization record via your DNS provider** button (needs [DNS Provider Push](./dns-provider-push.mdx) configured for that domain's provider) alongside the record to add manually.

## MTA-STS and TLSRPT

These two are about transport security rather than sender authentication - they don't affect whether DMARC passes or fails, but they're part of the same "is this domain's mail properly locked down" picture:

* **MTA-STS** (RFC 8461) tells receivers to require a valid, verified TLS connection when delivering mail to your domain, closing the door on downgrade/interception attacks against unencrypted SMTP. dotMARC can host the policy file for you.
* **TLSRPT** (RFC 8460) is the reporting half - senders report back when a TLS connection to your domain failed, so you find out about a broken MTA-STS policy or expired certificate instead of mail just silently failing to arrive.

See [MTA-STS](./mta-sts.mdx) for the full setup: the one-time hosting infrastructure, the CNAME per domain, and what each status on the checklist means.

## Putting it all together

For a typical monitored domain, fully green means:

| Record | Where | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SPF | `<domain>` TXT | Declares which servers may send for this domain |
| DKIM | `<selector>._domainkey.<domain>` TXT | Lets receivers verify signed mail wasn't tampered with |
| DMARC | `_dmarc.<domain>` TXT | Declares policy and where to send aggregate reports |
| DMARC authorization | `<domain>._report._dmarc.<mailbox-domain>` TXT | Authorizes reports to be sent to a mailbox on another domain (skip if same domain) |
| MTA-STS | `mta-sts.<domain>` CNAME + hosted policy | Requires verified TLS for inbound mail |
| TLSRPT | `_smtp._tls.<domain>` TXT | Reports TLS delivery failures back to you |
